The problem

A river gauging station has 15 discharge measurements (called gaugings), taken at stages between 0.6 m and 3.0 m. The stage h is the water level at the station; the discharge Q is the flow, in m³/s. A bridge waterway must be designed for a flood stage of 5.0 m. Engineers describe such a station by a rating curve

Q = a (h − h₀)b

where h₀ is the stage at which the model says the flow would be zero. It is never known exactly, so it must be found from the data. You will fit the curve, see what goes wrong if h₀ is simply set to zero, and report the flood discharge with an honest ±.

How to complete this project

  1. Fit the naive curve, taking h₀ = 0.
  2. Estimate h₀ by scanning a grid of values.
  3. Improve the fit with Gauss–Newton.
  4. Check the predictions on the three highest readings.
  5. Report the flood discharge at 5.0 m with an error bar, and judge how far to trust it.

Put all of this in one Python file, run.py, export your submission at the bottom of this page, and upload it.

Fixed definitions

Everyone must use exactly these definitions, so that every correct program gives the same numbers.

Quantities

  • Model. ln Q = ln a + b · ln(h − h₀), with natural logarithms. Fit by least squares with equal weights in ln Q. h₀ must stay below the lowest stage, min(h). b has no units; a has units (m³/s)/mb. Take logarithms of the numbers as given, with h in m and Q in m³/s.
  • Residual and SSE. The residual of reading i is ri = ln Qi − ln a − b · ln(hi − h₀). SSE is the sum of the squared residuals.
  • Flood discharge. Flood stage H = 5.0 m; flood discharge Q₅ = a · (H − h₀)b.
  • Leave-one-out error bar. Fit the curve 15 times, each time leaving out one reading (each refit repeats the scan and R1, below), and compute Q₅ each time. If Q₅ changes a lot, the prediction is sensitive to which measurements happened to be taken. Call the 15 values Q₅(i) and their mean Q̄. The error bar is ΔQ₅ = √( (14/15) · Σ (Q₅(i) − Q̄)² ). This is not the ordinary standard deviation of the 15 values: any two refits share 13 of their 14 readings, so the 15 values lie much closer together than independent estimates would, and the factor 14/15 (instead of the usual 1/14) scales them back up. The error bar is not a guarantee of how accurate the flood prediction is.

Numerical experiments

  • Scan grid. h₀ = 0, 0.001, 0.002, … m, up to the largest value that is at most min(h) − 0.001 m. Every dataset issued has its smallest SSE inside the grid, not at either end.
  • Gauss–Newton (Lecture 2, Appendix A7). Unknowns p = (ln a, b, h₀). At each iteration, find the step δ as the least-squares solution of J δ = −r. Here r is the vector of the 15 residuals and J is the 15 × 3 matrix of their derivatives with respect to (ln a, b, h₀); its three columns are −1, −ln(h − h₀) and b/(h − h₀). The new estimate is p + δ.
  • Step halving. While the new h₀ would be above min(h) − 10⁻⁶ m, or the new SSE would be larger than the current SSE, halve δ.
  • Stopping rule. Stop when the length (2-norm) of the step actually taken, after any halving, is below 10⁻¹⁰, or after 100 iterations.
  • Three runs. They test whether the answer depends on the starting guess, and whether step halving makes the method reliable.
    • R1 starts from the scan result: the full vector (ln a, b, h₀) of the line with the smallest SSE in part (b). With step halving.
    • R2 starts from (ln a, b, h₀) = (0, 1, 0). With step halving.
    • R3 starts from (0, 1, 0) without step halving. It may converge, leave the allowed region or fail; no marks depend on it. Your run.py must catch a failure (for example with try/except) and carry on, so that the results file is still written.

Sample test question

The individual test has 4 questions like this one on this topic. This sample will not appear on the test.

Why are equal weights in ln Q appropriate when each gauging has about 5% relative error?

A. A 5% relative error is about the same size in ln Q at every stage.
B. Large discharges matter more for design.
C. Equal weights always give the smallest flood estimate.
D. The logarithm removes all measurement error.

Answer

A. In ln Q, a 5% error is a shift of about 0.05 whatever the size of Q, so every reading deserves the same weight.

Words used here

  • Leave-one-out error bar: repeat the whole fit 15 times, each time leaving out one reading, and measure how much the answer moves.
  • Step halving: shortening a Gauss–Newton step that would leave the allowed region (h₀ below the lowest stage) or make the fit worse.
