The problem

A ready-mix supplier wants to predict the compressive strength of a concrete mix from its ingredients and its age, and a site engineer wants to know what extra water does to strength. You have real laboratory records: the UCI Concrete Compressive Strength data (I-C. Yeh, 1998), 1030 tested mixes. Each team gets its own 100 records for fitting and 200 separate laboratory records held back for checking.

You will compare a simple law with a physical basis against flexible models with many coefficients, and see why such models need ridge regression when only 100 mixes are available. Then you will ask what the fitted coefficients can and cannot tell you.

How to complete this project

  1. Fit two Abrams-type laws, one using water/cement and one using water/binder.
  2. Fit a linear model in all eight inputs and look at its conditioning.
  3. Fit a quadratic model, with and without ridge, choosing the ridge strength by cross-validation.
  4. See how much one coefficient jumps when the data change, with and without ridge.
  5. Predict a new mix and the effect of extra water, and judge what that effect means.

Put all of this in one Python file, run.py, export your submission at the bottom of this page, and upload it.

Fixed definitions

Everyone must use exactly these definitions, so that every correct program gives the same numbers.

Quantities

  • Inputs. Eight inputs, in this order: cement, slag, ash, water, superplasticiser, coarse, fine, ln(age). Their names for part (b) are cement, slag, ash, water, superplasticiser, coarse, fine, ln_age. Binder = cement + slag + ash. The output f is the measured strength, in MPa.
  • Check error. The root-mean-square of (predicted strength − measured strength) over the 200 check records, in MPa.
  • Correlation. The ordinary correlation coefficient between two inputs over the 100 fitting mixes.
  • Condition number. The 2-norm condition number of the 100 × 9 linear design matrix (intercept column included), as numpy.linalg.cond gives it.
  • Spread. The sample standard deviation (dividing by 9) of the 10 half-sample results.

Models and numerical rules

  • Abrams-type laws. Ordinary least squares of ln f on the columns [1, water/cement, ln(age)], and separately on [1, water/binder, ln(age)]. Predict f = exp(fitted ln f), with no other correction.
  • Linear model. Columns [1, the 8 inputs]. Quadratic model. Columns [1, the 8 inputs, the 36 products xixj with i ≤ j taken in input order (the 8 squares and the 28 cross products)]: 45 columns, built from the unscaled inputs. Both predict f in MPa directly, with no logarithm.
  • Scaling. Every column except the intercept: subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation (dividing by the number of rows), both computed on the rows being fitted. Apply the same numbers to any rows you predict.
  • Ridge. With A the scaled design matrix and c its coefficients (c₀ is the intercept), minimise Σ(f − Ac)² + λ·Σ cj², where the second sum leaves out the intercept. The candidate values are λ = 10−3, 10−2.5, …, 10³ (13 values).
  • Cross-validation. For each fold k = 1 to 5: scale and fit on the 80 fit rows not in fold k, and predict the 20 rows in it. The score of λ is the total squared error over all 100 predictions. Choose the λ with the smallest score; if two scores agree to a relative 10⁻¹², take the larger λ. Then scale and fit on all 100 rows, and use the check rows once.
  • Half-samples. Use the linear model with the scaling computed once from all 100 fit rows. On each half-sample, fit by ordinary least squares and by ridge with λ = 10. The water coefficient is the coefficient of the scaled water column, in MPa per one standard deviation of water over the 100 fit rows.
  • Extra water. A hypothetical model calculation, not a mixing instruction: for the new mix, increase water by 10 kg/m³ in the inputs, keep every other input fixed, and take the change in predicted strength.

Sample test question

The individual test has 4 questions like this one on this topic. This sample will not appear on the test.

Why are the input columns scaled before a ridge fit?

A. Scaling puts the coefficients on comparable scales before the same penalty is applied to all of them; without it, each input's units would decide how strongly it is penalised.
B. Scaling makes the fit exact.
C. Scaling removes the correlation between inputs.
D. Ordinary least squares needs scaled inputs.

Answer

A. A coefficient's size depends on its input's units (kg/m³ against days, say); scaling puts every input on the same footing before they are penalised alike.

Words used here

  • Ridge regression: least squares with a penalty on the size of the coefficients, which keeps them from swinging wildly when inputs move together.
  • Cross-validation: judging a choice (here λ) by repeatedly fitting on part of the data and predicting the part left out.
  • Half-sample: a random half of the fitting mixes; refitting on several halves shows how much a result depends on which mixes happened to be tested.

Data source: I-C. Yeh (1998), "Modeling of strength of high-performance concrete using artificial neural networks", Cement and Concrete Research 28(12); UCI Machine Learning Repository, Concrete Compressive Strength.
